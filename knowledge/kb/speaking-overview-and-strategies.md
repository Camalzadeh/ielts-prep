# Speaking Test Overview and Strategies

This page brings together three things: the format of the test, what the teacher told the group about preparing for Speaking, and general strategies for each part. The general strategies are standard public IELTS advice and are labelled that way. They are not quotes from the course.

## 1. The test at a glance (standard IELTS format)

| Part | Time | What happens | What it tests most |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Part 1**: Introduction and interview | 4–5 min | The examiner checks your ID, then asks short questions on 2–3 familiar topics (work/studies, hometown, home, and then topics such as mirrors, watches or parks). | Answering naturally and quickly, everyday vocabulary |
| **Part 2**: Long turn (cue card) | 3–4 min | You get a card ("Describe a…" plus 4 bullet points), **1 minute** to prepare with paper and pencil, then you speak for **up to 2 minutes**. The examiner may ask one or two short follow-up questions. | Speaking at length, organising ideas, narrative tenses |
| **Part 3**: Discussion | 4–5 min | Abstract questions linked to the Part 2 theme, about people, society, the past and the future. | Giving and supporting opinions, comparing, speculating |

The whole test takes **11–14 minutes**, is face to face (or by video call), and is recorded. It is marked on four equally weighted criteria: **Fluency and Coherence**, **Lexical Resource**, **Grammatical Range and Accuracy**, and **Pronunciation**. Band descriptors have their own page in the knowledge base.

The difference between the parts matters. The course's *Vocabulary for writing* handout puts it this way: *"In the Speaking test, the examiner will always ask you both about society in general (in Speaking part 3) and also about your personal life, country and background (in Speaking parts 1 and 2.)"* The same handout notes that contractions (*don't*), personal stories and less formal words such as *really* or *good* are fine in Speaking, even though they should be avoided in Writing Task 2.

## 3. What the teacher's model answers teach (patterns in her material)

- **Part 1, three sentences.** Give a direct answer, then a reason or detail, then an extra point, example or contrast. That takes about 20–30 seconds. Example (*Is that a big city or a small place?*): "I would say it is a medium-sized city that is growing very fast because of new businesses. While it does not feel as crowded as a massive capital city, it still has everything you need… It gives you the advantages of city life without the terrible traffic."
- **Opening softeners** buy thinking time naturally: *To be completely honest…*, *Generally speaking…*, *Without a doubt…*, *I have to admit that…*, *If I had to pick one downside…*, *From what I have seen…*.
- **Linkers for adding and contrasting:** *Also / Plus / On top of that / Additionally / However / While… / On the other hand*.
- **Every answer has 2–3 collocations.** These are the vocabulary bullets under each answer, such as *steady job*, *gain practical experience*, *tight deadlines*, *prime location*. Learn them in chunks, not as single words.
- **Part 2: vocabulary grouped by type.** Each cue card comes with *idioms*, *collocations*, *phrasal verbs* and *advanced words*, for example *to recharge one's batteries*, *a hidden gem*, *to wind down*, *awe-inspiring*. Aim to use 1–2 from each group.
- **Part 3, six to seven sentences.** State your view, give reason 1, give reason 2 or the other side, add an example, then close with *Ultimately / Therefore / In short*. These answers reuse the Part 2 idioms, so the Part 2 vocabulary pays off twice.

## 4. Strategies by part (standard IELTS guidance)

### Part 1
- Never answer with only "Yes" or "No". Use the answer, reason, example pattern, and stop after 2–3 sentences. Part 1 rewards natural, relaxed answers, not speeches.
- Match the tense to the question: *Did you…as a child?* needs past forms, and *Have you ever…?* needs present perfect plus a past-simple detail.
- Paraphrase the question instead of repeating it: *Do you like parks?* → "Yes, I'm quite a fan of green spaces…"
- If you don't have a strong opinion, say so and explain why. Honesty sounds more fluent than an invented story.
- You may ask *"Could you repeat the question, please?"* In Part 1 the examiner can only repeat it, not explain it.

### Part 2
- Use the full minute. Write keywords for each bullet, not sentences, plus 3–4 phrases you want to use.
- Structure: a one-sentence introduction ("I'd like to talk about…"), then the bullets in order, then spend the most time on the last *why / how you felt* bullet.
- Keep talking until the examiner stops you. If you run out early, add a comparison (*then vs now*), a short story with a specific moment, or what you would do differently.
- Use narrative tenses for past events (past simple, past continuous, past perfect: "I had never got up that early before") and future forms for plans ("I'm planning to…", "I'd love to…").
- If the topic doesn't fit your life (e.g. you never had a tall building in your town), you may adapt it or invent believable details. You are marked on language, not truth.

### Part 3
- Give a clear position first, then develop it: reason → example → consequence or contrast.
- Talk about people in general, not only yourself: *many people*, *the younger generation*, *in my country*, *in the past… whereas nowadays…*
- Use hedging and speculation: *It depends on…*, *I'd say that…*, *This is probably because…*, *In the future, it is likely that…*
- It's fine to buy time: *"That's an interesting question; I've never really thought about it, but…"*. You can also ask the examiner to rephrase. In Part 3 they are allowed to.
- Aim for 40–60 seconds per answer. The examiner will interrupt if they want to move on, and that is normal, not a bad sign.

### For all parts
- **Fluency over perfection.** Self-correct quickly and keep going, and avoid long silences. Natural fillers (*well*, *let me think*, *actually*) are fine in moderation.
- **Pronunciation:** stress the key words, use chunks (*a hidden gem*, *at the eleventh hour*) so your rhythm sounds natural, and speak at a normal pace.
- **Don't recite memorised answers.** Examiners lower scores for rehearsed speech. Use the model answers to collect ideas and phrases, then answer in your own words.
- **Record yourself** (the app's Speaking section does this), then listen back for long pauses, repeated words (*very*, *good*, *thing*), and tense errors.
